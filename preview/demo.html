The coppersmiths who draw batik in metal

Before wax touches the cloth, someone has to bend the pattern out of copper. In Pekalongan, fewer people know how every year.

By Laras Wijayanti · 14 Oct 2026 · 3 min read

  2. Wax and a steady hand2

Text size19 px

Darsono’s workshop is a roofed yard behind his house, ten minutes by motorbike from the centre of Pekalongan. On the bench lies a sheet of paper with the parang pattern, the diagonal knife-blade motif, traced in pencil. Beside it lie a coil of copper strip, pliers worn bright at the tips and a soldering iron warming on a charcoal stove. He has made batik stamps here since 1987.

A stamp, called a cap, is how batik is made by hand in quantity. Instead of drawing wax onto the cloth line by line with a canting, a worker dips the copper stamp in hot wax and presses it onto the fabric, repeat after repeat. A good stamper covers a long cloth in under an hour;1 the same cloth drawn by hand takes weeks.

A drawing in copper

Every line of the pattern becomes a strip of copper standing on its edge. Darsono cuts each strip to length, bends it with the pliers to follow the pencil and solders it to a frame, so that the face of the stamp is a field of thin walls two centimetres deep. The dots are short pieces of wire, set one by one. A fine stamp, twenty centimetres square, can hold more than a thousand pieces.

“The paper tells you the shape,” he says, “but the copper tells you if it will print.” A wall that leans by half a millimetre leaves a thicker line in the wax. When a stamp is done he files its face flat on a whetstone and tests it on scrap cloth until the print comes out even.

Wax and a steady hand

In the stamping room of a batik house the wax, called malam,2 is kept liquid in a wide pan. The stamper dips the cap, shakes off the excess and sets it down with one firm push, matching each impression to the edge of the last. A good stamper’s repeats do not show; a careless one leaves seams.

Darsono’s stamps go to about a dozen batik houses in the city and in nearby Batang. A simple one costs Rp750,000. A fine one with two faces, one for each side of the cloth, costs Rp3.5 million and takes him three weeks.

Who still orders a cap

Since UNESCO listed Indonesian batik in 2009,3 more of it is sold than ever, but fewer stamps are ordered. Much of the cloth sold as batik is now screen-printed with batik patterns and never touches wax, and the houses that still stamp by hand keep their old caps for decades. “A stamp lasts thirty years if you clean it,” Darsono says. “A person who can make one takes ten.”

He had four apprentices in the 1990s. Now he has one, his nephew Fajar, who is twenty-three and delivers food on a motorbike in the evenings. Fajar can bend a border on his own; the fine fills he still leaves to his uncle. “He asks why I set the dots by hand,” Darsono says. “I tell him a machine would not know where they go.”

Notes

1A kain panjang, the long wrap cloth, is about two and a half metres. A twenty-centimetre stamp is pressed on it some sixty times on each side.
2Malam is a mix of paraffin, beeswax and pine resin. Each batik house keeps its own proportions.
3UNESCO added Indonesian batik to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity on 2 October 2009, now National Batik Day.
